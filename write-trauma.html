Understanding Yourself Is Not the Same as Working on Yourself

Trauma explains how you got here. It does not explain why you're still here.

— mwangi  · 23rd June 2026

I read an article recently about avoidant attachment. It was beautifully written, like genuinely. The kind of writing that makes you feel like someone finally put language to something you have always felt but never found the words for. It talked about fear, about walls built for survival, about the way childhood shapes how we receive love as adults. It was empathetic and poetic and I understood every word of it.

It also left me feeling some type of way.

Not because it was wrong. But because of what it left out entirely. The person on the other side of all that beautiful, explained, well-articulated avoidance. The one who showed up, stayed consistent, tried to be safe enough, tried to be patient enough, and still got left. That person does not get a poem written about their inner world. They just get to be collateral damage in someone else's healing arc.

Here is the thing about trauma that I think we have gotten dangerously comfortable misunderstanding, it is an explanation, not an exemption.

Knowing why you do something is not the same as deciding to stop doing it. Understanding the root of your behaviour is the beginning of the work, not the completion of it. But somewhere along the way just somewhere between therapy language going mainstream and the internet turning psychology into content, we started treating the explanation as if it were the receipt. As if being able to name what happened to you automatically clears the tab of what you have done to others because of it.

"Trauma is the reason you are the way you are. It is not the reason you get to stay that way."

It doesn't work like that. You do not get to hurt people and then hand them a detailed psychological breakdown of your attachment style as compensation. The breakdown might be accurate. It might even be moving. But it does not undo the hurt. And more importantly, it does not count as growth.

What bothers me most is how this kind of thinking quietly removes choice from the equation. If everything you do is a trauma response, if every wall is just survival, if every act of self-sabotage is just your nervous system protecting you, then where exactly do your decisions live? Where does the part of you that chose to ghost someone, to pull away, to blow up something good because stillness scared you, where does that part take any ownership?

Because you did choose. Maybe not consciously. Maybe not easily. But you are not a passive passenger in your own behaviour. You have a mind that can observe itself. The very fact that you can read an article about avoidant attachment and recognise yourself in it is proof of that. Recognition is a choice point. What you do after you recognise yourself is where character actually lives.

"The people around you did not give you your wounds. So why are they the ones absorbing them?"

There is a version of self-awareness that is just self-absorption wearing a more acceptable name. It looks like growth from the outside. The therapy speak, the introspective captions, the ability to explain your patterns with frightening accuracy. But if the only person that self-awareness ever serves is you, if it functions mainly as a shield against being held accountable, then it is not healing. It is just a more sophisticated way of staying exactly where you are.

I want to be clear about what I am not saying. I am not saying trauma is not real. I am not saying healing is easy or linear or something you can simply decide your way out of. I am not saying people who struggle with avoidance or fear of intimacy are bad people. Most of them are not. Most of them are genuinely trying to make sense of themselves the best way they know how.

But trying to understand yourself and actively choosing not to change, those two things can't coexist. And we do not talk about that enough. The comfort of having an explanation can become its own trap. Because once you have the explanation, you have to decide what to do with it. And doing something with it is harder than just holding it.

Trauma explains the cage. It does not lock it. At some point, not instantly, not without help, not without difficulty, but at some point, you have to decide whether you are going to live in the explanation or do something about it. Not for the people you have hurt. Not even for the people you might hurt next. But because you are inside the cage too, and understanding how it was built is not the same as walking out of it.

The most honest thing I can say is this, your story matters. What happened to you matters. And the people in your life deserve someone who understands that mattering and being accountable are not opposites. They are the same work.
